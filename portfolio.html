Digital health  ·  mixed methods  ·  2021–present
Sugar Slay: research behind a gamified decision-support app for Type 1 diabetes
Founder & Lead Researcher  ·  Mith.AI / Sugar Slay (Northeastern startup)
Question

How do people with Type 1 diabetes, and the clinicians and caregivers around them, actually make daily management decisions, and where could a gamified tool genuinely help rather than add burden?

Method

End-to-end research program: a structured discovery effort of 110+ semi-structured interviews across patients, clinicians, caregivers, and payers; a moderated, task-based usability study of the app; and survey design and analysis of self-reported outcomes.

Interviews (110+)Usability testingSurvey design Thematic analysisBehavioral data
Finding

Three studies converged on a clear picture. In the ML evaluation on the OhioT1DM benchmark, a Sequence-to-Sequence BiLSTM gave the most consistent glucose forecasts across subjects (mean RMSE 21.9 mg/dL at 30 minutes), which set the prediction engine. Usability testing (7 participants) showed strong acceptance of the glucose-forecast feature (71% would use it daily) but flagged that the initial gamification display felt overwhelming. And need-finding with 6 caregivers surfaced a core tension: supporters wanted safety without surveillance, preferring "status at a glance" and gentle nudges over raw data streams.

Impact

The research directly shaped the product. The forecast findings drove a progressive-unlocking redesign of the gamification and clearer prediction explanations; the caregiver insights became an entire companion app, Sugar Slay Care, built around role-based, user-controlled data permissions rather than always-on monitoring. The work was published as a first-author paper in Frontiers in Digital Health and supported by ~$86K in non-dilutive funding (Northeastern SPARK, MassVentures Acorn, NSF i-Corps).

Applied analytics  ·  stakeholder research  ·  2024
Organizational outcomes analysis for a nonprofit
Data Analyst (Volunteer)  ·  Asperger/Autism Network (AANE)
Question

How do outcomes differ between current and past clients, and what does that tell leadership about where the organization is and isn't serving people well?

Method

Independently designed and executed a cohort analysis of 15+ years of private-pay LifeMAP coaching data (2008–present; 213 active vs 1,165 past clients) across 28 metrics. Integrated data from multiple sources, performed extensive cleaning and de-identification, ran the quantitative analysis, and produced a comprehensive report with professional visualizations for a non-technical leadership audience.

Cohort analysisData cleaning & anonymization Descriptive & inferential statsRetention curves Data visualizationStakeholder reporting
Finding

The active cohort was far more deeply engaged than past clients (median 47 sessions vs 14; 86.85% pursuing multiple goals vs 61.55%), and a retention-style curve showed active clients remaining in coaching well above past rates at every time point. Engagement was highly concentrated: the most-engaged segment (50+ sessions) was about 46% of active clients but roughly 84% of all coaching hours. Employment, Life Skills, and Independent Living were the dominant goal areas. The analysis also surfaced survivorship and data-quality caveats (missing gender and session-timestamp fields) that shaped how the results should be read.

Impact

The findings are now informing budgeting and marketing strategy for the LifeMAP program, which generates roughly 30% of the organization's revenue and underwrites many free and low-cost services for the autistic community. This analysis had been a long-standing organizational priority left uncompleted due to its complexity; I designed and delivered it end to end and presented to both the program team and the executive team.
